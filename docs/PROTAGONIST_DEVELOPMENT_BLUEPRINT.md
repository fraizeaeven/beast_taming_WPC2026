# Protagonist Development Blueprint — Phase 1

> **Project:** Beast Taming  
> **Development route:** D — Complete Fantasy Saga  
> **Working approach:** Editorial-led; author retains final approval  
> **Current focus:** Protagonist psychology and transformation  
> **Status:** Core psychological combination approved by the author on 2026-11-28. Supporting details below remain proposals unless explicitly marked approved.  
> **Canon policy:** This document supplements the Story Bible. It does not silently resolve undecided worldbuilding, family history, names, mechanics or plot events.

## 1. Development decision record

### Author-approved direction

The author approved the recommended combination of three psychological layers:

1. **Primary wound — A: The boy who wants to be worthy.** His central insecurity is conditional worth: he believes he must become valuable enough that others cannot discard or disregard him.
2. **Defensive behaviour — B: The boy who refuses to be controlled.** In response to his low status and lack of agency, he develops a strong resistance to being ordered, manipulated or treated as property. This is a defensive response, not necessarily his deepest wound.
3. **Eventual moral dilemma — C: The boy who fears becoming cruel.** As he gains power, he must confront the possibility that he is reproducing the strength-first cruelty that hurt him.

**Editorial synthesis:** A is the wound; B is the armour; C is the moral consequence if the wound and armour remain unexamined.

These three layers should not be treated as three unrelated character arcs. They are connected expressions of one underlying struggle: how a person who learned to measure worth through power can learn to choose what power is for.

### Still undecided

The author has not yet approved the protagonist's name, exact personality, appearance, initial cultivation level, natural talent, immediate opening-scene objective, specific behavioural habits, voice, first beast, or the precise events that trigger each stage. These remain open for subsequent development.

## 2. Existing established character facts

The following facts are carried forward from `docs/STORY_BIBLE.md` and remain controlling:

- The protagonist is 16.
- He belongs to the Dark Family.
- He is the patriarch's eleventh child and the third son of the fourth concubine.
- His mother was formerly a talented beast tamer whose cultivation was crippled.
- He is bullied and looked down upon because of the disfavoured status attached to his mother.
- The patriarch is cold, ambitious and strength-focused, while secretly protective of each of his children.
- At least one sibling secretly resents the protagonist.
- The Dark Family's hidden potential is the ability to cultivate beasts of all seven elements.
- The Dark Family was betrayed by the other six families and remains bound by a promise made by a former family head.
- The story challenges the apparent moral binary: darkness may conceal goodness and light may conceal corruption.
- The earlier plot in which the patriarch gambles an ancestral artifact is dropped and must not be reintroduced as current canon.
- The ancient beast soul remains an earlier working direction only. Its identity, selection mechanism, relationship and introduction are undecided.

## 3. Core character concept — proposed

**Working logline:** A sixteen-year-old boy raised to feel replaceable tries to become so useful and powerful that nobody can disregard him, only to discover that the pursuit of invulnerability may turn him into the kind of person he once feared.

This concept is intended to make the protagonist emotionally legible before his hidden inheritance or extraordinary abilities become important.

### Psychological architecture

| Layer | Proposed interpretation | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Primary wound | He experiences his place and worth as conditional. | Approved at concept level |
| Defensive behaviour | He resists control and tries to preserve agency. | Approved at concept level |
| Eventual moral dilemma | He fears becoming cruel or strength-obsessed like those who hurt him. | Approved at concept level |
| External desire | Gain sufficient strength and standing to secure his future and protect his mother. | Proposal |
| Deeper desire | Be recognised and accepted without having to prove his worth. | Proposal |
| Core fear | Being powerless when someone he loves needs him; being treated as disposable. | Proposal |
| Misbelief | "If I become valuable enough, nobody will be able to treat me as disposable." | Proposal |
| Coping mechanisms | Concealing emotional needs, observing social dynamics, over-preparing and proving usefulness. | Proposal |
| Potential flaw | Confusing self-worth with usefulness and strength. | Proposal |
| Potential strength | Attentiveness to what others overlook, endurance and an ability to read social situations. | Proposal |
| Moral danger | Treating people as tools or dismissing the weak because he once felt disposable himself. | Proposal |
| Ultimate need | Understand that worth is not earned through power, usefulness or another person's approval. | Proposal |

**Important nuance:** The protagonist should not be written as universally kind, endlessly patient, secretly perfect or automatically correct. He may feel envy, pettiness, resentment, shame and the desire to retaliate. His moral identity is revealed by the choices he makes with those feelings, not by the absence of ugly feelings.

## 4. The contradiction that creates dramatic tension

**Proposed defining contradiction:** He claims he wants independence, yet secretly craves recognition from the people whose judgement he says he does not value.

Possible expressions (illustrative behaviour, not fixed scenes):

- He says he does not care about his father's approval, but notices when his father praises another child.
- He claims he does not need his siblings, yet remembers who excluded him and who remained silent.
- He wants to protect his mother, but may feel ashamed that he cannot offer her security.
- He wants to make his own decisions, yet initially measures success using the household's definition of strength.
- He resents being controlled, but may voluntarily accept unfair burdens in the hope that someone will finally recognise his usefulness.

This contradiction should create behaviour that exposes what dialogue tries to conceal.

## 5. Seven-stage transformation arc

The seven stages describe a long, uneven transformation. They are not a rigid checklist that must each occupy a single plot event. Regression is possible; a later crisis may reactivate an earlier defence.

### Stage 1 — Denial: "I don't need their approval."

- **Conscious position:** He insists that household status and recognition do not matter to him.
- **Unconscious behaviour:** He notices invitations, praise, resource allocation, family seating and who is listened to.
- **Possible thought:** "I was only checking who was there."
- **Emotional mixture:** Longing, embarrassment, resentment and self-protective indifference.
- **Physical expression:** A glance toward the person whose recognition he claims not to want; a posture adjustment; a brief tightening in the jaw.
- **Narrative function:** Let the reader detect the need before he admits it.

### Stage 2 — Recognition without change: "I know how this family works. I just need to become stronger."

- **Conscious position:** He recognises that approval influences him but believes the solution is to become powerful enough to command respect.
- **Unconscious behaviour:** Accepts unfair tasks, overworks, suppresses complaints and waits to be noticed.
- **Emotional mixture:** Determination, humiliation, hope and resentment.
- **Physical expression:** Fatigue ignored; clenched hands; shallow breathing when he sees another person rewarded.
- **Narrative function:** His coping strategy produces short-term gains, reinforcing the misbelief.

### Stage 3 — Suffering: "Why am I still not enough?"

- **Conscious position:** He has made progress, but the recognition he expected does not arrive or is offered only conditionally.
- **Unconscious behaviour:** Compares himself with siblings, replays dismissive remarks and studies the achievements of favoured children.
- **Emotional mixture:** Envy, exhaustion, disappointment, shame and anger.
- **Physical expression:** A hollow sensation in the stomach, a tight throat, tension behind the eyes or restless sleep.
- **Narrative function:** Demonstrate that external progress cannot automatically heal the internal wound.

### Stage 4 — Considering change: "Maybe I have been chasing the wrong thing."

- **Conscious position:** He begins to suspect that his own judgement and desires may matter more than the household's approval.
- **Unconscious behaviour:** Pauses before accepting an order; considers what he wants before responding.
- **Emotional mixture:** Fear, curiosity, guilt and fragile self-respect.
- **Physical expression:** Hesitation, an unfinished response, tension that does not immediately become obedience.
- **Narrative function:** Make autonomy feel unfamiliar rather than instantly liberating.

### Stage 5 — Realisation: "My silence is hurting people too."

- **Conscious position:** He recognises that his coping strategy and choices can harm people beyond himself.
- **Unconscious behaviour:** Initially rationalises his actions, then hears an excuse resembling the justifications used by people who mistreated him.
- **Emotional mixture:** Guilt, defensiveness, grief, anger and fear of self-recognition.
- **Physical expression:** Avoided eye contact, a defensive posture, delayed speech or an inability to meet the gaze of someone affected.
- **Narrative function:** Establish that suffering does not automatically make someone virtuous.

### Stage 6 — Effort: "I can choose differently."

- **Conscious position:** He begins setting boundaries and acting according to chosen principles.
- **Unconscious behaviour:** The old urge to seek approval still appears, but he can recognise it before obeying it.
- **Emotional mixture:** Anxiety, resolve, grief for lost opportunities and cautious hope.
- **Physical expression:** A visible pause, controlled breathing, deliberate refusal or a difficult apology.
- **Narrative function:** Change costs him something. Doing the right thing is not always rewarded.

### Stage 7 — Transformation: "I decide what my strength is for."

- **Conscious position:** He no longer needs the family to validate his existence.
- **Unconscious behaviour:** He can stand before someone powerful without instinctively measuring his worth against theirs.
- **Emotional state:** Greater self-respect, clarity and capacity for chosen loyalty; not emotional invulnerability.
- **Physical expression:** Less compulsive self-monitoring; a steadier response to status and intimidation.
- **Narrative function:** He can still pursue strength, protect his mother and challenge the existing order, but these actions arise from chosen values rather than desperation for approval.

**Transformation is not indifference:** He may continue to want affection, recognition and family connection. The change is that these desires no longer define his worth or dictate his moral choices.

## 6. Behavioural and emotional expression guide

Use this sequence when writing emotionally important scenes:

1. **Trigger:** What exactly happened, and what does he perceive it to mean?
2. **Automatic reaction:** What does his body or attention do before conscious thought?
3. **Interpretation:** What does he tell himself the event means?
4. **Emotion:** Identify the mixed emotions, including contradictory ones.
5. **Physical sensation:** Show where and how the emotion manifests in the body.
6. **Visible expression:** What can another character observe?
7. **Concealment:** What does he hide, minimise or pretend not to feel?
8. **Choice:** What does he actually do?
9. **Consequence:** What does the choice cost him or someone else?
10. **Continuity:** Does the response match his current arc stage and relationship history?

Avoid repeating one stock response (clenched fists, lowered eyes, jaw tension) in every scene. Physical expression must vary with context, personality, fatigue, fear, social risk and the person in front of him.

### Illustrative situation A — Someone humiliates his mother

- Automatic reaction: His fingers curl before he consciously decides to move.
- Possible thought: He wants to retaliate but calculates the consequences.
- Emotional mixture: Anger, shame, helplessness and protectiveness.
- Physical sensation: Heat behind the ears, tight throat, pressure in the chest.
- Visible expression: Unnatural politeness, silence or a restrained smile.
- Hidden behaviour: He remembers who laughed and who remained silent.

**Editorial caution:** Restraint can be intelligent or cowardly. The narrative should let the reader distinguish between the two.

### Illustrative situation B — His father praises another son

- Automatic reaction: He straightens and pays close attention.
- Possible thought: He wonders what the other son did differently.
- Emotional mixture: Envy, disappointment and longing.
- Physical sensation: A brief hollow feeling in the stomach.
- Visible expression: Indifference.
- Hidden behaviour: He may later imitate the praised son's training or study his achievements.

### Illustrative situation C — His mother asks him not to fight

- Automatic reaction: Anger subsides, but frustration turns inward.
- Possible thought: He wonders whether she sees him as incapable.
- Emotional mixture: Love, frustration, guilt and helplessness.
- Physical sensation: His shoulders loosen while his chest remains tight.
- Visible expression: An easy smile and a promise to understand.
- Hidden behaviour: He may train harder, believing strength will remove her need to worry.

These are writing patterns, not approved plot events or mandatory behaviours.

## 7. Relationship pressure design

Relationships should challenge different parts of his psychology. Supporting characters must have their own desires, contradictions, boundaries and agency; they must not exist only to teach the protagonist lessons.

### Mother — love, protection and autonomy

**Established:** Formerly talented beast tamer; cultivation crippled.

**Proposed dramatic function:** He wants to protect her, but may confuse protection with deciding what is best for her. Her own pride, history, opinions and wishes must remain meaningful. She should not be reduced to a fragile person waiting to be rescued.

**Potential conflict:** Her request that he avoid a fight may be interpreted by him as doubt in his ability, even if her actual motive is fear for his safety.

### Father — approval, authority and inherited values

**Established:** Cold, ambitious and strength-focused; secretly protective of each child.

**Proposed dramatic function:** The father embodies the recognition the protagonist claims not to want. His secret protectiveness must not erase the harm caused by coldness, unequal opportunity or tolerated household hierarchy.

**Potential conflict:** The father may protect his children from external threats while accepting a harsh internal hierarchy because he believes it prepares them to survive. This remains a proposal, not an established explanation of his behaviour.

### Resentful sibling — mirror and rival

**Established:** At least one sibling secretly resents the protagonist.

**Undecided:** Identity, age, gender, exact motive, conduct and eventual role.

**Proposed dramatic function:** The sibling can mirror the protagonist's own insecurity. Their conflict should arise from specific history and competing needs, not a generic jealousy label. Do not select a motive without author approval.

### Other siblings — varied loyalties

Their exact identities and positions remain open. Avoid making all siblings uniformly cruel or uniformly supportive. Each should have independent incentives, fears and interpretations of the household.

## 8. The moral danger: becoming what he hates

As the protagonist gains strength, authority and the ability to command powerful beasts, his old belief may mutate into a justification for cruelty:

- "They are weak. They need to become stronger."
- "I cannot waste resources on useless people."
- "Sentimentality will destroy the family."
- "I am doing this for everyone's survival."

The danger is not that he becomes an obvious villain overnight. It is that he can rationalise harmful decisions using the same strength-first logic that once harmed him.

He may begin to resemble his father in manner, policy or judgement. This should be a genuine moral confrontation, not a simplistic reveal that the father was secretly evil.

**Central moral question:** Can he wield strength without reproducing the cruelty that shaped him?

## 9. Connection to the beast-taming premise — thematic proposal

The protagonist's psychology offers a potential thematic bridge to beast taming:

**Can he build a genuine bond with a creature that has its own will if he still believes strength determines whose wishes matter?**

This creates a useful distinction between:
- Command and cooperation.
- Obedience and trust.
- Possession and partnership.
- Fear-based compliance and chosen loyalty.

A beast should remain an individual with needs, preferences, fears, goals and the ability to resist. The protagonist's growth should affect how he treats that individual, while the beast's independent choices should also challenge him.

This is a thematic recommendation only. No first beast, species, rank, personality, contract mechanic, ancient soul or event has been selected or made canon by this document.

## 10. Editorial guardrails

1. Do not make him universally admirable. Let him be jealous, petty, defensive, wrong and occasionally unfair.
2. Do not equate trauma with virtue. His suffering explains some behaviour; it does not excuse harm he causes.
3. Do not make strength instantly solve emotional problems.
4. Do not make the father’s secret protectiveness erase his responsibility for harmful choices.
5. Do not reduce the mother to a motivation object.
6. Do not decide the resentful sibling's motive before developing that sibling independently.
7. Do not turn the ancient beast soul or all-seven-elements inheritance into a substitute for personality.
8. Do not resolve the seven-family mystery prematurely to validate the protagonist.
9. Do not force linear growth. Old defences can return under stress.
10. Give every major emotional change a trigger, choice, consequence and later behavioural evidence.
11. Keep the protagonist's voice, habits, humour, competence, blind spots and preferences distinct from his psychological wound.
12. Let other characters sometimes be right about him, even when they have treated him unfairly.
